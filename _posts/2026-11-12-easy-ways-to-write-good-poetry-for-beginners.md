*By [Skyler]({{ "/about/" | relative_url }}), poet and English Literature graduate*

You're sitting in your room with a pen and paper, wanting to write a poem, but you don't know what to write. As a beginner, it's even more troublesome. So you look for inspiration and try to stay motivated.

Inspiration does come to beginners too. But it doesn't show you how to start a poem or what to write in the first line, and it mostly works right away for poets who have written for long. So beginners end up writing nothing. This is the beginner's struggle.

There are many ways to write poetry. In this article, you'll see the easiest yet sophisticated way to write good poetry, step by step.

## What is good poetry?

Good poetry expresses what the poet truly feels, in the poet's own way. It doesn't need perfect rhyme or big words.

There is no single right or wrong in poetry. Whatever a poet decides to write is poetry. Still, good poetry usually has three things:

- **Honest feeling.** It comes from something the poet really feels.
- **Your own way of saying it.** How the poet says it matters more than whether every reader understands it.
- **A connection.** A reader may feel something different from what the poet meant, or may not understand it entirely. The connection can still be there.

If it comes from you and it's honest, I'd say it counts.

## How to write poetry in English: five easy steps

These five steps show you how to write poetry in English. They work in any language:

1. Start with the emotion you already carry.
2. Write the weight of the emotion, not the explanation.
3. Replace plain words with exact ones.
4. Try free verse.
5. Write imagery as it is, without explaining it.

Don't worry if your English isn't perfect. You can look up words as you go, and I'll show you how in step 3.

## 1. Start with the emotion you already carry

Emotion is something you already have, so you don't have to go searching for a topic. It can easily boost you into writing something.

The emotion you carry might be something you can explain, or it might not. Either way, it can start a poem.

Try this: before you open a blank page, ask yourself, "What am I feeling right now?" Write that one feeling at the top. That's your poem's starting point.

### Good topics to write poetry about

Pick an emotion from your past, or one you carry now. Here are some good topics to start with:

- A feeling you can't name
- Someone you miss
- A place you left
- A small joy
- A fear
- A memory that won't fade

The feeling doesn't have to be new. It can be something from years ago that you still carry. If it's still with you, it's still worth writing.

## 2. Write the weight of the emotion, not the explanation

You don't have to explain what the emotion is, and you don't have to explain why it came to you. But write the weight of the emotion.

Here is the difference. The lines on the right are from my poem [The Phantom Sound]({{ "/poems/the-phantom-sound/" | relative_url }}):

| Explaining | Showing the weight |
| --- | --- |
| "I'm tired because my mind never stops." | "My head is stuffed with mud." |
| "I'm scared of my own thoughts." | "...as if a pack of wolves were chasing me to devour my body." |

The first line gives a reason. The second gives the reader the feeling itself. Readers don't need your whole story. They need to feel how much it weighed.

Writing the weight lifts the way you talk about your emotion. So don't try to explain yourself. Just tell the reader how heavy it felt, and let them feel it.

## 3. Replace plain words with exact ones

When we write something, most of the time we don't know the exact words for what the emotion carries. So we plainly put "so happy" or "so sad." Instead, we can use words like ecstasy and agony, in place of very happy and very sad:

| Plain words | Exact words |
| --- | --- |
| very happy | ecstasy, elation |
| very sad | agony, sorrow |
| very angry | fury, rage |
| very scared | dread, terror |

Words like ecstasy and agony exaggerate. They show the intensity of a feeling, whether you were too happy or too sad.

"Very sad" tells the reader you were sad. "Agony" tells them how much. The exact word also helps readers understand you faster.

### How to find the right word

Choosing these words can be hard, because you are supposed to know the word to replace it with. My tip is the internet and a dictionary. It is completely fine to look for words that say exactly what you are trying to say.

1. Write the feeling in plain words first.
2. Describe the situation in one sentence, even if it's messy.
3. Search the internet or a dictionary for words that fit it, such as "word for feeling very sad."
4. Pick the word that carries the weight you meant, and check its meaning before you use it.

Keep it honest: a bigger word is not always a better one. Choose the word you understand and that fits the feeling.

### An example from my poem Nebula

When I wanted to talk about a specific group of people in my poem [Nebula]({{ "/poems/nebula-the-human-nature/" | relative_url }}), I used the phrase "blue bloods":

> Those bluebloods with radiant skin,<br>
> Believed they would glow forever.

"Blue bloods" could mean a lot of things. It also means rich kids: wealthy children with very nourished health and a nourished life. Just like that, you can find words in a dictionary or on the internet to say what you want to say.

## 4. Try free verse

If you want to know how to write good free verse poetry, start here. Free verse is one of the easiest ways for beginners to start. You don't have to worry about how many words you have in a line, how many lines you have, or how many stanzas you have. You have a lot of freedom, so I highly recommend it for beginner poets.

### How to write good free verse poetry

1. Start with a line from your feeling.
2. Break the line wherever you want. A short thought can be a short line.
3. Stop when the feeling stops. Rhyme and meter can come later, if you want them.

It's like talking to a friend instead of reading from a rulebook. The poem can take whatever shape your feeling takes. The [Poetry Foundation](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/education/glossary/free-verse) describes free verse as lines that closely follow the natural rhythms of speech.

## 5. Write imagery as it is, without explaining it

Imagery is easy to understand, and yet it can be carefully built by the poet. When you can visualize something and put it into words, you don't have to explain the sight you are looking at, the feeling you sense or the smell you notice.

It doesn't have to be well explained in a poem. That is the most important thing: you don't explain it, you just write it how it is, as it is. People often mistake imagery for saying everything about the thing you are seeing in the scene. But you can just add those objects in one line, and then say how it felt.

For example:

> One table<br>
> Three chairs<br>
> One cup of coffee<br>
> The air felt cold<br>
> Who's going to drink it?

A question at the end could be nice, like "Who's going to drink it?" You don't have to think too much about visual imagery. Just write it as it is and how it felt.

### Add an adjective

Even in imagery, adding an adjective adds a lot. It's still up to the poet: you can say how it is, and then add an adjective to give more. I think this is a very important and kind of advanced technique in visual imagery. An adjective about the scenario gives readers an amazing visual idea.

So look around you, or remember a place, and write only what is there. Then add one adjective and see how the picture changes.

## How to write the first line in poetry?

To write the first line of a poem, choose your theme and your technique first. Then start from the moment that matters most to you: where the feeling began, where it ended, or right now. Write one simple, honest line, and let the poem grow from it.

### Choose your theme and feeling

Your theme is what the poem is about. It could be a happy moment from today, something from the past, something you're looking forward to, a person, a tragedy or an adventure. Every one of these carries a theme.

Your feeling is how that theme feels, and it is usually more specific than "happy" or "sad." Happy can become ecstasy. Sad can become melancholy. A poem can also carry more than one feeling, like melancholy and internal conflict.

### Choose your technique

A technique is the way you tell the poem. It doesn't have to be difficult, and you don't need to study many techniques to start. For example, you can show only the images of a place, like a cold room and a cup on a table. Or you can describe a small, quiet detail while something big is happening around it. Pick the way that helps readers connect with you. To learn more, read [What Is a Poetic Technique?]({{ "/2026/11/01/what-is-a-poetic-technique.html" | relative_url }})

### Write the first line

With your theme, feeling and technique chosen, pick a starting point:

- **Where it began.** Sit and think about where it all started.
- **Where it ended.** Start from the end and look back.
- **A calm moment.** Give yourself a minute to think of a calm time when everything made sense.
- **The present.** Write what is happening now, then bring the reader into the past, the future, or what you admire.

### An example from my poem

The first line of my poem [The Phantom Sound]({{ "/poems/the-phantom-sound/" | relative_url }}) is "My head is stuffed with mud." The feeling is exhaustion and internal conflict. The technique is one simple image. It doesn't explain anything. It shows how heavy the feeling is, and the rest of the poem follows from it.

## Writing poetry examples from my own poems

Here is a short example from my poem [The Eternal Sunshine]({{ "/poems/the-eternal-sunshine/" | relative_url }}). It's free verse, it never explains the feeling, and the imagery does the work:

> Crawling on the floor<br>
> Slipping through the bedsheet<br>
> Foggy, cold and cozy<br>
> I closed my eyes, saw the light<br>
> Why didn't you tell me?

Notice the adjectives: foggy, cold and cozy. Nothing is explained, and the last line is a question. The reader feels that something is wrong without being told.

Read it slowly, one line at a time. Each step does one small job, and together they make a poem.

## Try it in five minutes

1. Write one feeling you have right now.
2. Write two lines about how heavy it feels, without saying why.
3. Add one line of imagery: list what you see, as it is.
4. Swap each plain word, like "very" or "so," for one exact word.
5. Read it aloud. Don't count words or lines. Free verse lets the poem be as long or short as it needs.

That's a poem draft. It doesn't have to be finished or perfect.

Don't judge it while you write. Finish it first, and fix the words later.

## One last thing

There are many things in poetry that can feel complicated for a beginner. There are things a beginner may not be able to write yet. But the first step is writing. And you will always wonder what to write. That's why you pick an emotion.

In life, a person comes across so many emotions, and it can be really revolutionary to express those feelings. Sometimes it also helps the writer heal and connect with others through their imagination. So poetry can bring many good things to a beginner who starts from emotion.

Nothing is wrong, and nothing is right. Whatever a poet decides to write is poetry. Whether you're a beginner or advanced, always express yourself. We're human, and we express ourselves in many ways. That should be allowed.

So start small. One feeling and a few lines, and you have already begun. Write your first line today.
