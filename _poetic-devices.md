# 101 Poetic Devices with Examples {#poetic-devices}

A poetic device is a tool a poet uses to shape sound, meaning and feeling. This page explains 101 of them in simple words, with a short example for each. Use the list below to jump straight to any device.

## Sound {#sound}

Sound devices shape how a poem is heard. Read each example aloud.

### Alliteration {#alliteration}

Alliteration is the repetition of the same consonant sound at the start of nearby words. It makes a line feel musical and easy to remember, and it can also set a mood. Soft sounds like "s" can feel quiet, while hard sounds like "b" or "k" can feel sharp. Poets use alliteration to guide your ear and to link ideas together. Notice the sound, not the letter: "knight" and "night" start with the same sound.

**Example:** "When to the sessions of sweet silent thought" (William Shakespeare, Sonnet 30). The repeated "s" gives the line a hushed, thoughtful feel.

### Assonance {#assonance}

Assonance is the repetition of the same vowel sound in nearby words. The consonants around the vowel can be different. It is a subtle device: you may not notice it, but you can feel it. Assonance adds music to a line, can help it flow, and can add a gentle or dreamy mood, depending on the sounds around it. Like alliteration, it works by sound, not spelling, so "bean" and "seem" share a vowel sound, while "bear" and "bean" do not.

**Example:** "Hear the mellow wedding bells" (Edgar Allan Poe, "The Bells"). The short "e" sound in "mellow," "wedding" and "bells" gives the line its soft, ringing music.

### Consonance {#consonance}

Consonance is the repetition of the same consonant sound in nearby words, wherever it falls: at the start, in the middle or at the end. When the repeated sound is only at the start, we usually call it alliteration. Consonance can make a line feel tight and musical, and it can echo a mood: repeated "t" or "k" sounds can feel hard, while "l" and "m" can feel smooth. Poets use it to link words together and to make a line stick in the reader's memory without using rhyme.

**Original example:** "The slick black rock cracked at dusk." The repeated "k" sound in "slick," "black," "rock" and "cracked" ties the line together.

### Sibilance {#sibilance}

Sibilance is the repeated use of hissing sounds, such as "s," "sh," "z" and soft "c." It is a special kind of alliteration or consonance. Sibilance can sound like whispering, wind, rain or a snake, so it often creates a hushed, secret or eerie mood. Because the sound is so noticeable, use it carefully: a little can be magical, but too much can sound like hissing. Many poets use it to bring quiet scenes to life.

**Example:** "And the silken, sad, uncertain rustling of each purple curtain" (Edgar Allan Poe, "The Raven"). The hissing "s" sounds make the room feel hushed and uneasy.

### Onomatopoeia {#onomatopoeia}

Onomatopoeia is a word that sounds like the thing it names. Words like "buzz," "hiss," "crash" and "murmur" copy a real sound. In poetry, these words help the reader hear the scene, not just read it. Onomatopoeia makes writing lively and sensory, and it can set the speed or mood of a line: "tick" feels small and steady, while "boom" feels huge. Use it when you want a sound to be part of the picture.

**Example:** "From the jingling and the tinkling of the bells" (Edgar Allan Poe, "The Bells"). "Jingling" and "tinkling" imitate the light, repeated sounds of bells.

### Euphony {#euphony}

Euphony is a pleasant, smooth and musical sound in a line of poetry. It is often created with flowing consonants like "l," "m," "n" and "r," long vowels and a smooth rhythm. Words that flow easily off the tongue can create euphony. Poets use it for calm, beautiful or loving moments, because the sound itself feels gentle. If a line sounds pleasing and harmonious when read aloud, it may be euphony. It is the opposite of cacophony.

**Example:** "The moan of doves in immemorial elms, / And murmuring of innumerable bees" (Alfred, Lord Tennyson, "The Princess"). The soft "m," "n" and "r" sounds make the lines hum.

### Cacophony {#cacophony}

Cacophony is a harsh, jarring mix of sounds in a line of poetry. It often comes from abrupt, clashing consonants like "k," "t," "g," "b" and "d" next to each other, so the line sounds harsh or discordant. Poets can use cacophony on purpose to suggest chaos, anger, noise or pain, so the sound matches the meaning. When a line sounds bumpy or clashing when you read it aloud, it may be cacophony. It is the opposite of euphony.

**Original example:** "The crash of crates and the creak of the cart." The hard "k" sounds clatter like the noise they describe.

### Rhyme {#rhyme}

Rhyme is when words have matching sounds. In perfect rhyme, the sounds match from the last stressed vowel onward, like "star" and "are." It gives poetry a pattern, makes it easy to remember, and creates a pleasing feeling of things clicking into place. Rhyme is not required in poetry, but it is one of its oldest tools. Poets use it to link ideas, to build rhythm and to surprise the reader with the words they chose to pair. Rhyme can be perfect, like "bright" and "night," or imperfect.

**Example:** "Twinkle, twinkle, little star, / How I wonder what you are!" (Jane Taylor, "The Star"). "Star" and "are" rhyme, so the lines feel complete.

### End rhyme {#end-rhyme}

End rhyme is rhyme at the end of lines. It is the most common kind of rhyme. When the last words of two lines rhyme, the reader feels a small click, and the lines feel connected. Poets use end rhyme to build a pattern, like AABB or ABAB, and to give a poem a song-like feel. It also helps readers remember lines. End rhyme lives at the line breaks, so where you break a line decides where the rhyme falls.

**Example:** "Tyger Tyger, burning bright, / In the forests of the night" (William Blake, "The Tyger"). "Bright" and "night" rhyme at the ends of the lines.

### Internal rhyme {#internal-rhyme}

Internal rhyme is rhyme that happens inside a line, rather than only at the end. Two words in the same line rhyme, or a word in the middle of a line rhymes with a word at the end. It can give a line extra music and speed it up, and it can make a poem feel like a chant or a song. Use it when you want a line to bounce along, but do not overdo it, or the poem may start to sound like a nursery rhyme.

**Example:** "While I nodded, nearly napping, suddenly there came a tapping" (Edgar Allan Poe, "The Raven"). "Napping," inside the line, rhymes with "tapping" at the end.

### Slant rhyme {#slant-rhyme}

Slant rhyme, also called near rhyme or half rhyme, is when words almost rhyme but not perfectly. They share a similar vowel or consonant sound, like "shape" and "keep." Poets use slant rhyme to avoid the sing-song feel of perfect rhyme, and it can create a slightly uneasy, unfinished feeling. It can suit sad, serious or uncertain subjects, because the sounds never fully settle.

**Example:** "It seemed that out of battle I escaped / Down some profound dull tunnel, long since scooped" (Wilfred Owen, "Strange Meeting"). "Escaped" and "scooped" match their consonants but not their vowels, a kind of slant rhyme called pararhyme.

### Eye rhyme {#eye-rhyme}

Eye rhyme is when words look like they rhyme on the page but sound different when spoken. "Love" and "move," or "cough" and "bough," are classic examples. The rhyme is for the eye, not the ear. Eye rhymes often appear because pronunciation has changed over the centuries, so some pairs may once have rhymed. Poets can also use them on purpose, for a small, playful mismatch between what you see and what you hear.

**Example:** "...Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds, / Or bends with the remover to remove" (William Shakespeare, Sonnet 116). "Love" and "remove" look alike but sound different.

### Rhyme scheme {#rhyme-scheme}

A rhyme scheme is the pattern of rhymes in a poem. We label it with letters: each new end sound gets a new letter, and a repeated sound repeats the letter. If lines 1 and 2 rhyme, and lines 3 and 4 rhyme, it is AABB. If lines 1 and 3 rhyme, and lines 2 and 4 rhyme, it is ABAB. To find it, label the last word of each line.

**Example:** "The curfew tolls the knell of parting day, (A) / The lowing herd wind slowly o'er the lea, (B) / The ploughman homeward plods his weary way, (A) / And leaves the world to darkness and to me. (B)" (Thomas Gray, "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard"). The pattern is ABAB.

### Refrain {#refrain}

A refrain is a line, phrase or word that is repeated at intervals in a poem, often at the end of a stanza. It works like the chorus of a song. A refrain creates rhythm, stresses an important idea and gives the reader something to hold on to. Its meaning can change each time it appears, as the poem builds around it. Use a refrain when one idea or feeling keeps returning, like grief, longing or obsession.

**Example:** "Quoth the Raven, 'Nevermore.'" (Edgar Allan Poe, "The Raven"). The repeated word ends many stanzas and feels darker each time.

### Repetition {#repetition}

Repetition is using the same word, phrase or sound more than once. It is one of the simplest and strongest devices in poetry. Repetition adds emphasis, builds rhythm and shows strong feeling, like urgency or obsession. It also makes poems easier to remember. The key is purpose: repeat a word because it matters, and let the repeat change the meaning a little each time. Refrain, anaphora and epistrophe are all special kinds of repetition.

**Example:** "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, / Creeps in this petty pace from day to day" (William Shakespeare, Macbeth). The repeated word makes time feel slow and endless.

### Anaphora {#anaphora}

Anaphora is the repetition of the same word or phrase at the start of several lines, sentences or clauses. It builds a strong rhythm, like a drumbeat, and gives the passage power and emphasis. Poets use anaphora to list things, to build up feeling and to make an idea feel unstoppable. It is common in speeches and prayers as well as poems. When you notice several lines starting the same way, you have found anaphora.

**Example:** "This royal throne of kings, this sceptred isle, / This earth of majesty, this seat of Mars" (William Shakespeare, Richard II). Each phrase begins with "This," piling up praise.

### Epistrophe {#epistrophe}

Epistrophe is the repetition of the same word or phrase at the end of several lines or sentences. It is the end-position counterpart of anaphora, which repeats at the start. Because the repeated words come last, they stay in the reader's ear, so epistrophe is great for a strong, memorable finish. It adds rhythm and weight, and it can make an idea feel final. It is common in speeches and in poems that circle back to one central thought.

**Example:** "Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them, / Cannon in front of them" (Alfred, Lord Tennyson, "The Charge of the Light Brigade"). Each line ends with "of them." (The lines also begin with "Cannon," which is anaphora.)

### Rhythm {#rhythm}

Rhythm is the pattern of strong and weak beats in a line of poetry. It is the beat you feel when you read aloud, like the beat in music. Rhythm comes from stressed and unstressed syllables, line length, pauses and repeated sounds. A steady rhythm can feel calm or marching; a broken rhythm can feel nervous or wild. To find rhythm, read the line aloud and tap along with the stresses. Meter is a more exact way to measure it.

**Example:** "Once upon a midnight dreary, while I pondered, weak and weary" (Edgar Allan Poe, "The Raven"). The line follows a mainly strong–weak pattern: ONCE up-ON a MID-night DREAR-y.
